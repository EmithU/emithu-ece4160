Lab 4

This lab seeks to set up open-loop control of the car.

Prelab

In order to enable open-loop control, we need to integrate the microcontroller, batteries, ToF and IMU sensors, and motor drivers into the car. In theory, we could probably get away with using just one battery to power both the microcontroller/sensors and the motors. In practice, the motors require an overwhelming amount of current to operate, significantly more than anything else on the car. Furthermore, motors tend to cause voltage fluctuations along the lanes that they're connected to. Connecting the microcontroller to a different power source prevents brownouts, better ensuring that we have some control of the car no matter what it is doing.

Below is a full wiring diagram of the car, with all connections included. Note that pins 12, 13, 15, and 16 are used for controlling the motor drivers. Additionally, the motor drivers each have 2 channels; I used a solder bridge to hook them together, increasing the max current supplied to the motors.

There is a chance (from the manufacturer) that each of our motors rotate at different rates, requiring us to fix this offset in software so that our car can move in straight lines. Unfortunately for me, I have motors with this behavior. Below is a video of the robot moving before any calibration.

I wanted to correct this before finding the limits of the PWM values I can input, so (for now) I picked a PWM value both motors could work with, 200. The car drifts to the left over time, but not too significantly. This indicates that the right set of wheels are rotating at a faster rate than the left. Through trial and error, it was found that setting the PWM of the left wheel to 0.94x of the right wheel allowed the car to move straight, as demonstrated below.

In particular, I created SET_PWMA and SET_PWMB Bluetooth commands to let me rapidly guess and check. Once I found my multiplier of 0.94x, I created a SET_PWM command to let me set both motors at once; it is pictured below.

With this, I wanted to find the lower and upper bound of PWM values I could input before the motors refuse to work. A 100% duty cycle (input value of 255) was found to work on both motors, leaving the lower bound to discovery through trial and error. It was found that the two motors seemed to have different lower bounds; perhaps this helps to explain the need for a calibration factor earlier on.

In particular, when letting the wheels spin freely (and not on a surface), one pair of wheels spinned unhindered while the other seemed to need more power to start. Because we previously determined that the car is capable of moving in a straight line for at least 6 feet, this difference is largely negligible for now, but might come into play for labs 9-12 when we need the car moving very slow.

If we assume that both wheels require the same starting PWM to move, a value of 50 (~20%) seems sufficient. Assuming this is most likely fine for labs 5-8, but this will probably have to be revisited for labs 9-12.
