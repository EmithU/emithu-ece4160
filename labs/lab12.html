High-Level Plan

With this, we can develop an actionable plan. At a high level, here is what I do:

1. Have the car start at the same known location and orientation at every trial, directly facing the next target.

2. Measure and input the ToF value corresponding to when the car is on the next target. Have the car move forward until it's on the target.

3. Rotate until we're facing the next target, and repeat step 2 for the next target. Rinse and repeat steps 2-3 until we are back at the start.

This sounds simple enough, but only works effectively if all utilized controls and filters are precise and accurate; a lot of tuning and adjusting is in order. Due to limited lab hours, I made a custom arena and primarily developed there. Everything pertaining to movement in my system is closed-loop, so running it in the lab is just a process of inputting different ToF and angle values. As such, most 'in progress' videos/pictures/graphs are outside the lab, but I do include runs on the actual arena at the end of this report.

Development - Linear Control

I debated for a while on if I wanted linear control to be open-loop or closed-loop, in the sense that I have the car's PWM change dynamically as it gets closer to the desired ToF reading (like in lab 5). Open-loop control would offer more speed, but sacrifice on accuracy without significant tuning that would need to be re-done for every target. In my opinion, a moderately fast car that moves very accurately would be more impressive than a very fast car that is not very accurate. As such, I use my linear PID controller to approach the target.

The P and D terms go without saying (P for general speed, D to slow down as we reach the target). I originally wanted the I-term because I want the car to slow down significantly as it reaches it's target (to make ToF measurements less noisy when close and to provide more stability prior to rotations), but don't want this process to get too slow; in my mind, the I was a good compromise between P and D. In practice, P and D got the job done effectively, and introducing I just resulted in overshoot.

After significant tuning, I found that a kp = 0.17 and kd = 5.9 worked best. Below are the linear P/D graphs of the 1-turn run. Note that a bit of the graph is missing; this is when the car was rotating.

While the P values mostly make sense, the D values seem to be going crazy. This is because the Kalman Filter has slight imperfections whenever it is in an update step, and the D term is very sensitive to those imperfections. In theory, this can cause very unstable behavior (particularly in situations where the Kalman Filter is not as effective at predicting, like when the car is moving very slow). I found that, in practice, this was not causing real-world issues so I chose to ignore it.

Development - Orientation Control

For the 180 degree turn in lab 8, I used a strategy involving a 'settling time' between phases (to ensure that the motors are doing what the code wants them to do), a time limit (so the car moves forward even if not at the target), a range of acceptable angles (because I determined that the turn only needs to be close to 180 degrees, not exactly 180), and another 'settling time' that the car uses to verify that it is maintaining the desired angle before moving on (and not slipping out of angle). This resulted in very snappy, and surprisingly accurate, turns.

For this lab, I want to keep the settling times and the valid ranges, but remove the time limit. In lab 8, the car would exit it's turn phase either because it's in range or because of the time limit. I need this car to now achieve a precise heading, and so removing the time limit is necessary.

As for the valid range - I used 15 degrees in lab 8, which is way too much here. I still want an acceptable range though to maintain some of the snappiness in the turns. I stuck with a range of +/- 1 degree; this is tiny because slight inaccuracies in angle accumulate as the car performs more turns. This whole 'range' concept is me trying to take a shortcut in my orientation PID controller, to account for erroneous noise or random changes in dynamics. If my PID controller is reliable, this small range should still work and let rotations occur quickly, without performance varying unpredictably.

With more tuning, I found that a kp = 10.5, ki = 1.0, kd = 35.0, and integral wind-up = 80 worked best. I really, really wish rotations could have been more 'sloppy' so the car can drift around, but us hitting the points takes priority. So I made it slow and steady. When doing the real run, I began running into problems. Both of my PID controllers and the Kalman Filter worked very well here. I kept having to readjust the PWM offset between the motors in order to make the car move straight. This has been a reoccuring problem thorughout this course; no singular wheel factor works because the offset changes based on battery level. Normally, trials are short enough that I just manually find a value and stick with it. Here though, the car must travel for extended periods of time, and significant sources of error will build up and hurt the final trial tremendously. It was particularly malicious in this lab becuase the error in an even slightly incorrect offset built up over time.

This above problem single handedly turns my algorithm from a home run to essentially luck, and this bothered me to my core. Unbalanced motor controls change the angle at which the car apporaches a point, which messes with the angle that the car turns to, which corrupts everything. Not only that, but as the car moves, it uses battery, and so the car will become more unbalanced through a trial. I have never been more annoyed at something in my life. I got fed up with this and added a third PID controller, just to force the car to move straight.

This controller is conceptually similar to my orientation controller in that we are trying to reach and maintain a specific heading, and I established in labs 9 and 11 that it can make small angle corrections. As such, I model this code similarly to that code. I have separate kp/ki/kd/clamp values and found them experimentally. I would normally go into detail on my PID testing, but the process here is very similar to what I have done for previous labs (and earlier for this lab). I landed on a kp = 4, ki = 0.03, kd = 10, and integral wind-up clamp = 10.
